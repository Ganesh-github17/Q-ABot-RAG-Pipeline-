TITLE: Deep Learning Explained

SECTION: From Machine Learning to Deep Learning

Deep learning is a subfield of machine learning based on artificial neural networks that contain many layers of computation stacked on top of one another, which is where the word "deep" comes from. Traditional machine learning algorithms often require a human expert to manually design features, for example deciding that the edges, curves, or textures in an image might be useful for recognizing an object. Deep learning largely removes this manual step: given enough labeled data, a deep neural network can learn to extract useful features directly from raw input, such as pixel values or raw audio waveforms, on its own.

This ability to automatically learn hierarchical representations is the main reason deep learning has become the dominant approach for tasks such as image recognition, speech recognition, and natural language understanding over the past decade. Early layers of a deep network tend to learn simple patterns, such as edges in an image or common short sequences of letters in text, while deeper layers combine these simple patterns into progressively more abstract concepts, such as recognizing a face or understanding the sentiment of a sentence.

SECTION: The Artificial Neuron and the Neural Network

The basic building block of a neural network is the artificial neuron, loosely inspired by biological neurons in the brain. Each artificial neuron receives one or more numerical inputs, multiplies each input by a learned weight, adds a bias term, and passes the result through a nonlinear activation function such as the rectified linear unit, commonly abbreviated ReLU, or the sigmoid function. Neurons are organized into layers: an input layer that receives the raw data, one or more hidden layers that perform intermediate computation, and an output layer that produces the final prediction. A network with many hidden layers is called a deep neural network.

The process of adjusting the weights and biases of a neural network so that it produces accurate predictions is called training, and it relies on an algorithm called backpropagation combined with an optimization method such as stochastic gradient descent. Backpropagation computes how much each weight in the network contributed to the overall prediction error by applying the chain rule of calculus, and the optimizer then adjusts each weight slightly in the direction that reduces the error, repeating this process over many iterations across the training dataset.

SECTION: Convolutional Neural Networks

Convolutional neural networks, often abbreviated CNNs, are a specialized type of deep neural network designed primarily for processing grid-like data such as images. Instead of connecting every neuron in one layer to every neuron in the next layer, a convolutional layer applies a small set of learnable filters that slide across the input, detecting local patterns such as edges, corners, or color gradients regardless of where they appear in the image. This design, called weight sharing, dramatically reduces the number of parameters compared to a fully connected network and makes the model naturally capable of recognizing a pattern no matter where it occurs in an image, a property known as translation invariance. Stacking many convolutional layers, often interspersed with pooling layers that reduce the spatial size of the data, allows a CNN to build up a hierarchy of visual features, from simple edges in early layers to complex object parts and whole objects in later layers. CNNs are the workhorse architecture behind most modern computer vision systems, including those used for object detection and medical image analysis.

SECTION: Recurrent Neural Networks and the Rise of Transformers

Recurrent neural networks, or RNNs, were historically the dominant architecture for processing sequential data such as text, speech, or time series, because they maintain an internal memory, called a hidden state, that is updated as they process each element of a sequence one at a time. Variants such as long short-term memory networks, or LSTMs, were developed to help RNNs remember information over longer sequences. However, because RNNs process data one step at a time, they are slow to train on long sequences and struggle to capture very long-range dependencies.

The transformer architecture, introduced in 2017, addressed these limitations using a mechanism called self-attention, which allows the model to directly compare every element of a sequence with every other element in parallel, rather than processing the sequence step by step. This parallelism made it possible to train much larger models on much larger datasets, and transformers quickly became the dominant architecture not only for natural language processing but also, in modified form, for computer vision and other domains. Almost all modern large language models, including the models used for chatbots and text generation, are built on the transformer architecture.

SECTION: Training Deep Networks in Practice

Training deep neural networks in practice requires attention to several practical issues beyond the basic backpropagation algorithm. Large networks are prone to overfitting, so practitioners use regularization techniques such as dropout, which randomly disables a fraction of neurons during training to prevent the network from relying too heavily on any single neuron, and data augmentation, which artificially expands the training set by applying transformations such as rotations or crops to existing examples. Training very deep networks can also suffer from the vanishing gradient problem, in which the gradient signal used to update early layers becomes extremely small, making learning slow or unstable; architectural innovations such as residual connections, which allow information to skip over layers, were introduced specifically to address this problem and enabled the training of networks with over a hundred layers.

Training modern deep learning models, especially large language models, also requires substantial computational resources, typically specialized hardware called graphics processing units or tensor processing units, and can take anywhere from hours to months depending on the size of the model and dataset.

SECTION: Applications and Limitations

Deep learning powers a wide range of applications, including image classification and object detection in computer vision, machine translation and text generation in natural language processing, speech recognition, recommendation systems, and game-playing agents. Despite its successes, deep learning has well-known limitations. Deep models typically require large amounts of labeled training data to perform well, they can be difficult to interpret because their internal reasoning is distributed across millions or billions of numerical weights, and they can fail unpredictably on inputs that differ significantly from their training data, a problem sometimes called distribution shift. These limitations are active areas of ongoing research, alongside efforts to make deep learning models more efficient, more interpretable, and more robust.
